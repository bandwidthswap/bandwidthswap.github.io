3.7 Residual attacks

- Refusal to forward after accepting. Handled by conditional payment: the hop is never paid for what it did not forward. - A last hop that will not acknowledge. Only possible when payer and recipient are different parties. Endpoint services, where the payer is also the recipient, do not have this problem. Relay services are between members: the recipient is a bonded NYM, and a recipient that fails to acknowledge delivered chunks is slashed. 3.8 Alternatives considered

3.8 Alternatives considered

  • Validator probing. Mints push random payloads through relays and time them. Relays learn to recognize probe traffic and prioritize it, one pipe can masquerade as many nodes, and probes measure a relay's behavior toward validators rather than toward users. Rejected, and version 0.2's liveness check, which was the same thing by another name, is removed with the uptime penalty it served.

A.2 Windows and custody. On a link with a contact plan the window is the contact: it opens when the link does, closes when it does, and the receipt is per contact. The window is prepaid (Section 3.6). A relay that stores bytes during one contact in order to carry them to the next is in custody of them, and custody is nothing more than a window on the next link: the relay is paid for it by the next link's receipt, not by this one. Delay-tolerant networking removed custody transfer from the core of its bundle protocol for want of a way to make it reliable; this is that mechanism with the reason to accept custody attached. A relay accepts the bundle because the next contact's window is already paid for.

A.4 Payment. Below a light time the membership sets, the conditional payment of Section 3.2 applies, with lock timeouts no shorter than the route's round trip. Reconciliation. Mints reconcile their published commitments (Section 4) whenever a link between them exists, and reconciliation does three things. It is a check: a Mint whose commitments do not reconcile, or that has remitted to its Steward less than Section 3.3 requires, is shown to have done so from its own publications. It is a deposit: each Mint includes the other's latest commitment in its own, so that a receipt settled on one side is timestamped on the other, and a Mint that disappears leaves its register with the rest. And it is traffic: commitments cross a bridge in windows at the bridge's posted price like any other bytes, so a bridge that withholds reconciliation has declined a window, which is on the record and governed by the sending Mint's count, and a bridge that is the only path between two domains cannot hold their audits hostage without being seen to.

Exclusion. Across domains a Mint shown to have cheated is not slashed, because no other Mint can reach its bond. It is excluded: the honest Mint publishes the proof where any payer can find it before opening a window, stops reconciling, and slashes any relay bonded on its side that keeps settling with the condemned Mint. The bridge relays, bonded on both sides, carry the penalty at once. That is what their two bonds are for, and the membership sets them large enough that a bridge would rather lose one Mint than both. The penalty is worth the discounted value of every future reconciliation, which is much where reconciliation is daily and little where it is years, and on the longest links the two-sided bond carries the whole weight. A domain excluded for a proven fault may be readmitted when it has published a commitment that reconciles with the rest and remitted what it owed; a network that polices by closing doors has to be able to open them, or it is a cartel. The reconciliation protocol's wire format is open (Section 10). What it carries and what it is for are not.

A.7 What this does not change, and where it stops. What the protocol rewards: bytes a payer signed for and a relay carried. What it refuses to pay for: everything else. What proves delivery: two signatures on one receipt. Distance changes how long it takes to make one, not what it is, for as long as the route's round trip is inside the payer's horizon, the time the payer will still be there to receive the closed receipt. Inside the horizon the payer is the party that wanted the bytes, and the receipt tells it they came, which is the whole of Section 3.2. Past it the party that wanted the bytes cannot have paid, since the payment would have to precede the delivery by more than a lifetime. The sender pays, the recipient receives a gift, and the protocol is a sound way for an institution to pay relays per link toward silence, which is honest and is not a proof of anything to the party that wanted the bytes. The horizon is a property of the payer, not of the link. For a person it is a few decades of round trip, which puts the Moon, Mars and the nearest star inside it; for an institution it is the institution's life. On a link past a payer's horizon the window is opened by the sender, two-way traffic is two one-way ledgers, and the Mints net them at reconciliation (A.5). The Sybil condition of Section 3.3 holds on both sides of the horizon, because the fee is a ratio and the payer's loss is certain whoever the payer is: what the Steward took, it can return only below the cap.
